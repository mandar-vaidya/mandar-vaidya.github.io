Four ways AI projects go wrong And what I do about each
What happens

A stakeholder asks for a chatbot. What they need is for four teams to stop maintaining the same spreadsheet. The chatbot gets built, it works, and nobody uses it.

What I do: discovery before scoping. I interview the people who'd actually use the thing, trace the workflow as it really runs, and separate the request from the problem underneath it. Sometimes the answer isn't AI at all — and that's a finding worth paying for, not a failed engagement.

What happens

The team ships, reads ten outputs, and decides it seems fine. Three months later a prompt change quietly breaks 15% of cases and there's no signal that anything moved.

What I do: define acceptance criteria before the build starts, in language the business can sign off on. What counts as correct, what counts as unacceptable, and how you'll know next quarter. If you can't write it down, you can't ship it responsibly.

What happens

Models are confidently wrong in ways that pass every format check. I've watched one return plausible, well-formed financial figures that were entirely invented — no error, no warning, clean logs.

What I do: pressure-test feasibility before commitment. Where does a wrong answer cost you? What happens when the data source fails? I've built and run production systems myself, so when I tell you six weeks is optimistic, I've been on both sides of that estimate.

What happens

Every stakeholder adds one reasonable request. Individually they're all fine. Together they push delivery two quarters and nobody can point at the decision that did it.

What I do: hold the line, in writing, without losing the stakeholder. Changes get costed against the timeline before they're accepted. Saying no is most of the job, and doing it well is why people ask me back.

AI evaluation platform — the release gate

An enterprise GenAI evaluation platform I built as sole product owner, from concept to MVP. It became the mandatory go/no-go gate every AI model passes before production.

3models blocked from production
~65%less manual evaluation effort
<2 hrsreporting, down from days
45+ metricsRAGAgentsCode generationLLM-as-judgeDeepEval

Context

AI models were reaching production with no structured evaluation. Quality was judged by reading a handful of outputs, which meant regressions were found by users.

What I did

Defined 45+ metrics across quality, safety and compliance, prioritised the 10 highest-risk (hallucination, PII exposure, dangerous operations), and built the pipeline from test generation to executive reporting.

The hard part

Getting a metric set that the data, QA and business leads would all accept as the bar, then making it binding rather than advisory.

Why it matters to you

Evaluation stops being a feeling. A release either clears the bar or it doesn't, and you can show anyone why.

Context

Separate AI and RPA tracks, competing for the same business owners, with no shared view of which opportunities were worth funding.

What I did

Took three automations to production with three more in build, authored 15+ BRDs and process definitions, and built the use-case scoring framework and portfolio cost model leadership now funds against.

Prompt governance for a multi-agent portfolio

Architecture for governing prompts across nine production agents, so business owners control what changes before it goes live.

VersioningLineageApproval gatesDrift detection

Context

Prompts were edited directly in production. A one-line change could alter an HR or legal agent's behaviour with no record and no sign-off.

What I did

Designed versioning, lineage, approval gates and drift detection, giving HR, Legal and Supply Chain owners a controlled sign-off before any prompt change ships.

The hard part

Making governance light enough that teams use it rather than route around it.

Why it matters to you

Prompts are production code. If you can't say who changed one and why, you can't defend the output.

Staged rollout for AI outputs

A rollout model that lets teams reduce human checking of AI output only when the numbers justify it, not when the calendar says so.

Answer keysBack-testingShadow runsSampling

Context

Teams either kept full manual review forever, which killed the ROI, or dropped it on a launch date, which killed trust.

What I did

Designed four gated stages: held-out answer key, overnight back-test, shadow run with decaying human verification, then steady-state sampling. Each gate opens on measured accuracy.

The hard part

Agreeing the accuracy thresholds with business owners before the first run, so nobody renegotiates them after seeing results.

Why it matters to you

You get the automation savings without betting the business on an untested model.
