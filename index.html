Where AI projects actually break

Rarely in the code. These four show up in almost every engagement, and all of them are cheaper to catch before the build than after.

A stakeholder asks for a chatbot. What they actually need is for four teams to stop maintaining the same spreadsheet. The chatbot gets built, works fine, and nobody uses it.

What I do: talk to the people who'd use it, not only the people who asked for it, and trace the workflow as it really runs. Sometimes the answer isn't AI at all, and that's a finding worth paying for.

The team reads ten outputs and decides it seems fine. Three months later a prompt change quietly breaks one category of answers, and nothing tells anyone it happened.

What I do: agree acceptance criteria with the business before the build starts, then turn them into tests that run on every release.

Models are confidently wrong in ways that pass every format check. I've watched one return clean, well-formed financial figures that were entirely invented. No error, no warning, clean logs.

What I do: test against real inputs, edge cases and adversarial ones, and find out what a wrong answer costs you before a customer does.

Every stakeholder adds one reasonable request. Each one is fine. Together they push delivery back two quarters, and nobody can point to the decision that did it.

What I do: put a cost on every change before anyone says yes, and keep a running total everyone can see. Most requests get deprioritised by the person who made them.

The situation

AI models were reaching production with no structured evaluation. Quality was judged by reading a handful of outputs, so regressions were found by users.

What I did

Defined the metric set, prioritised the ten highest risks such as hallucination, PII exposure and dangerous operations, and built the pipeline from test generation to executive reporting across RAG, agents and code generation.

The hard part

Getting data, QA and business leads to accept one bar, then making it binding instead of advisory.

Why it matters to you

A release either clears the bar or it doesn't, and you can show anyone why. That's about 20 hours a week of manual checking you stop paying for.

The situation

Separate AI and RPA tracks competing for the same business owners, with no shared view of which opportunities were worth funding.

What I did

Ran discovery, wrote the business cases, and built the scoring framework and portfolio cost model leadership now funds against. One HR assistant case covered 8,000 employees and 45,000 tickets a year.

The hard part

Bringing two delivery tracks onto one cadence. The resistance came from a reasonable fear that AI would crowd out RPA work, and it had to be answered rather than overruled.

Why it matters to you

Most AI projects fail at definition. Scoping properly is where budget gets saved, usually more than the build itself costs.

The situation

Prompts were being edited directly in production. A one-line change could alter how an HR or legal agent behaved, with no record and no sign-off.

What I did

Designed versioning, lineage, approval gates and drift detection, giving HR, Legal and Supply Chain owners a controlled sign-off before any change goes live.

The hard part

Keeping it light enough that teams use it instead of routing around it.

Why it matters to you

Prompts are production code. If you can't say who changed one and why, you can't defend what the agent said.

The situation

Teams either kept full manual review forever, which killed the return, or dropped it on a launch date, which killed trust.

What I did

Designed four stages: a held-out answer key, an overnight back-test, a shadow run with decreasing human checks, then steady-state sampling. Each gate opens on measured accuracy.

The hard part

Agreeing the accuracy thresholds with business owners before the first run, so nobody renegotiates them after seeing the results.

Why it matters to you

You get the savings from automation without betting the business on a model nobody has tested.
